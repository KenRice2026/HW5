3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools

Honest answer: most of this board. The guardrails that kept the money safe live in the MCP tools, not in the team structure: pay refuses unapproved requests, create_purchase_order refuses blocked vendors, amounts come from the database, and drafts can't be sent. A single "shop clerk" agent with the same 26 tools and the same human-only approval would have been just as safe on these three tickets.

  • Ticket 102 is the clearest case. The whole job is get_lease(1) → get_cash_balance → request_payment_approval("rent", 1) → draft_message, then list_payments → update_ticket after you sign. That's about 6 tool calls. The team used 29 tool calls and 21 model requests across 4 agents, because each specialist starts cold: get_ticket ran 6 times and get_cash_balance 4 times.
  • The follow-up runs. 101's pass 3 (8343264a) only needed one updated draft, and 102's pass 2 only needed to confirm payment 1 and draft a confirmation. Waking the Boss to delegate a one-paragraph email to Customer Service is a manager forwarding a sticky note.
  • Where it costs the most: hand-offs. Every delegation has to re-explain the ticket in prose (the Boss's briefs on the ticket tabs are 3–6 sentences each), and the specialist then re-reads the database to verify it. That's why 101 cost ≈ 3× what a single agent would have needed.
  • What the team bought us:
    • Least privilege: only Accounting can file payments; only Customer Service can draft.
    • A readable audit: you can see who said what, as on the dashboard floor.
    • Parallel lookups on 103.
    • Specialised prompts that encode a role's rules, like Facilities treating email as a claim.
    These matter more as tickets get more ambiguous and risky. For three tickets, though, one well-prompted agent with role-checked tools (or a fixed workflow per ticket type) would have been simpler, cheaper and faster.

Tools: receive_purchase_order(po_id, qty_received) to raise inventory and close the order, advance_shop_date or a real clock, and a vendor-tracking feed (advance ship notices).
Agents: a Receiving agent that checks deliveries against orders (and flags short shipments), and a scheduler that wakes the Boss on due dates such as 9/5 for 101 and 10/2 for rent.
Result: 101 closes itself: receive → reserve for Tauhid → "ready for pickup" draft → resolved.
3. Talking to people for real. Five drafts are sitting unsent, draft 3 is stale, and the next ticket will arrive as an email nobody has typed into the board. By design, the team can only draft. It has no way to send, to read replies (did the club accept?), or to create tickets from the inbox. It also can't verify who's approving: "Ken Rice" was a typed name, and the Boss itself flagged that Claude signed on Ken's behalf. Stale drafts aren't detected automatically; Inventory only noticed draft 3 by luck. Tools: an email/Outlook connector with send_message behind a human approve-to-send queue (like the cheques), read_inbox and create_ticket, a check_draft_facts tool that re-checks a draft's quoted numbers against the database before it can be sent, and SSO sign-in for approvers.
Agent: a Communications agent that triages the inbox into tickets and sends approved drafts.
Result: draft 3's "vendor is blocked" line gets caught before the club ever sees it, and every approval is tied to a real login.

Bottom line

The team was correct and safe on all three tickets. It moved exactly $3,248.00 through three human signatures, never overdrew, and never sent a word without review. Where it fell short was efficiency (about $1.50 of model time to resolve $3,248 of bills and a $28 tee) and one quality slip (draft 3's stale reason). Both point to the same next step: keep the strict MCP guardrails and human cheques, but give routine tickets like 102 to a single agent with role-checked tools. Save the full team for tickets like 103 where judgement, pricing and stock pull in different directions. Then add the three things the shop really lacks: money in, goods in and messages out.
